## What?

Let's define what's happening in your brain. Or more precisely, what's happening in everyone's brain at some point, especially in high school. You might feel it as the weird feeling in your stomach when you want to get close to your crush.

### Difference from Real Love

Real love is knowing how messed up they look when they wake up. Knowing their trauma and history, knowing how they treat waiters and waitresses, and knowing their idiosyncrasies, ahem... unique behaviors, like how they chew with their mouth open, and then deciding to be with them regardless.

A crush is none of that. A crush isn't seeing the whole person. A crush is seeing only some that look good from a distance and inventing the rest, and then falling in love with the character you invented in your head instead. In psychology, this is called the Halo Effect (Dion, Berscheid, & Walster, 1972).

### A Scientific Name?

We call it a crush because we hear it everywhere. It's ubiquitous in any school, especially in the school where you currently reside, where you (hopefully) learn. But psychologists have a more precise name for this. They call it limerence. It's coined by the psychologist Dr. Dorothy Tennov in her book *Love & Limerence* (Tennov, 1979).

Don't feel bad, as most relationships start with a crush. How do you think your parents found each other?

## How do you know you're having a case of being smitten?

3. Being happy when they interact with you or being devastated when they don't.

In the modern age, there are specific scenarios to look out for. If you check for more than three of them, you have a crush.

1. You constantly check if they are online on a certain social media.

   - It activates the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA), which is the same area that activates when you consume cocaine.

4. You see who liked your post every 10 minutes, hoping that there's an off chance that your crush liked it.

5. You scroll on their Instagram page longer than needed.

## Why in the living hell does Limerence happen

For starters, you have to understand that limerence starts with yourself, not about them. The truth is, limerence is like a dormant virus. It activates when your brain is in the state of readiness.

Especially in high school, your brain is finding someone to be a protagonist—crush—of your life. Perhaps because someone is acting all lovey-dovey, it wants one itself. You might think you were smitten with your crush because they're the one, but they're not. More likely, they're just a fairly decent human being with a few positive traits that your brain latched onto.

To understand this, you have to understand gambling.

### What does gambling have to do with crushes?

That's a great question, and they have a connection. People gamble and lose their life savings. Why? Because of **intermittent reinforcement** (Skinner, 1948). It's basically sometimes getting a reward when a condition is met, like getting only a bit of money when you play a machine.

There's a study done by B.F. Skinner. She placed a pigeon in a cage with a button that dispenses treat. When it always dispenses treat, they only press it enough. When the button dooes noting, they obviously don't press it because they know that it won't do anything and end up starving. If the button occasionally dispenses a treat, the pigeon keeps pressing it, hoping for the next treat until their beak deteriorates (Skinner, 1948). They think, "What do I do to get the treat coming? Keep pressing? Press it differently? I WANT THE TREAAAAAATTTT!!!"

In this case, you are the pigeon.

### How it all connects

When your crush always replies every time or is always loyal, then the chase is solved. It's nice and warm. But when they take 5 hours to reply, you see them active, they like a post from you, or you think that there's a tiny chance of reciprocation, your brain becomes exactly like that desperate pigeon. You start "pressing the button" differently. You think,

- "Maybe if I wait 10 more minutes."

- "Maybe if I post this one song, they'll see it."

- "Maybe if I even change my profile picture!"

And so on.

Like a slot machine that pays out *sometimes*, when a person only gives signals sometimes, people will become obsessed with trying to win your attention.

## How a crush works

Once your brain has found someone that *may* have a chance of liking you back, even if it's delusional, your brain uses that as a basis and starts inventing stuff to fill in the blanks to get the maximum amount of dopamine every time your crush appears in your brain.

Your brain has the urge to fill in that blank because the behavior was hard-coded when we were simple primates. Ooga booga. I'm dead inside. Your ancestors can't just stop thinking about whether that rustle is the wind or a lion without resolving it first. Otherwise, they'd die. This is known as **Error Management Theory** (Haselton & Buss, 2000). It's better to be wrong and run away from the wind than be wrong and get your gene pool deleted. In the case of a crush, it's flipped. Instead of grave bodily injury, it's dopamine and getting your genes passed.

## Dopamine

Most people think that this is the pleasure chemical. They think it's the one that makes the brain go, "Mm... this is great. I'm happy!"

They're wrong.

Dopamine is the anticipatory chemical. It's the "Do it again" chemical. It's for cravings and the love of learning, not for satisfaction. That's called serotonin and other endogenous opioids. (Berridge & Robinson, 1998)

### The Anticipation

As I stated just now, you will get anxious when a notification pops up on your phone if you have a crush. That's the dopamine in action. It doesn't matter who notified you. You only care that there's a slight chance that your crush is the one that sent it.

> "Oh my! I really really hope it is my crush replying to me! Oh dear."

If it's actually them, your brain connects that dopamine spike to the notification sound. If it's just your mom, then the dopamine drops and you feel a bit down. Consequently, you go back to doomscrolling on your bed forever.

### Again!

Dopamine is evolution's creation to make sure we don't sit in a cave doing absolutely nothing. We'd die as we don't find food. It rewards repeatedly seeking.

- Find edible plants? DO IT AGAIN!

- Find a watering hole? DO IT AGAIN!

- Find a person who smiled at you once in a hallway? THAT'S THE ONE. DO IT AGAIN! AGAIIIINNNN!!!

### The Prediction Error

There is this thing called the Reward Prediction Error. Here's the gist of it.

- If you expect a text and get one, your dopamine is *meh*.

- If you don't expect a text and get one, your dopamine goes SKY HIGH!

- If you expect a text but don't get one, *blech.*

#### The Math Behind it

Think of it as like a numerical difference of expectations. This is represented by $\delta = R - V$ where $\delta$ is the prediction error, $R$ is the reward, and $V$ is the value.

- 10 (receiving a text) - 10 (expecting a text) = 0 (meh)  
this is why being stable can be boring.

- 10 (receiving a text) - 0 (not expecting anything) = 10 (YAY!)

- 0 (not receiving a text) - 10 (expecting a text) = -10 (NOOO!!)

- 10 (receiving a compliment through text) - (-10) (expecting a mocking remark) = 20 (Give me a moment as I process this one)

Your brain basically releases dopamine based on the *difference* in expectation. It's basically our brain's way to make us pay attention to anomalies. (Schultz, Dayan, & Montague, 1997)

### The Love of Learning

Dopamine is also related to novelty and learning. It's what drives (some of) us to continue learning Mathematics or Code. In this case, learning about your crush.

When you have a crush, they are like a puzzle.

- What does that emoji mean?

- Why are they wearing this shirt today?

- Do they like this specific snacks that I like?

Every time you solve a problem, your brain gives you a bit of a treat, aka dopamine. This is why you can spend four hours looking at their Instagram page from 5 years ago. Your brain thinks it's doing something important.

Your brain seeks out new information because new usually means probably useful for survival in the Stone Age, and even now. When you finally solve how crushes work, or a new detail about your crus, your brain treats it as a W. (Bromberg-Martin, Matsumoto, & Hikosaka, 2010)

### A. The Evolutionary View

Back in the days when all of us were chanting "ooga booga", being liked wasn't purely ego; it was for survival too.

If you got accepted into a tribe, you get food and protection. If you are lonely, get rejected, or kicked out from a tribe, you become a tiger's snack and your gene pool won't get passed.

When you get rejected by your crush, your brain isn't thinking, "Oh, they're probably busy," It thinks, "THE TRIBE HAS ABANDONED ME! I WILL GET EATEN BY A TIGER! I AM GOING TO STARVE! NOOOOO!!!"

This also explains why you have to look cool and impressive in front of your crush. Your brain thinks that your crush is like a tribe that gives protection.

### B. The Biological View

If you get rejected, it isn't just sadness; it is a withdrawal, like quitting smoking cold turkey (suddenly).

Your brain goes haywire. Instead of giving up, your brain goes into a state of frustration attraction, also known as adversity-heightened passion, as it is called in the paper. In a 2010 study, Fisher et al. found that looking at a rejecter continues to activate the reward center (VTA). The paper says when a reward is delayed, the dopamine neurons don't suddenly stop; they actually continue (Fisher et al., 2010).

## Jealousy

So, you think that jealousy is a social construct. The truth is it comes deepy from biology.

You see, jealousy is usually defined as a *state* caused by a perceived threat to a relationship and motivates behavior aimed at preventing and/or fighting the threat (Daly et al., 1982).

As documented by Daly, males in species with internal fertilization experience paternity uncertainty because they aren't the ones carrying the baby.

Different animals deal with this differently. For example, young cubs by other males are *systematically* killed, as in lions (Bertram, 1976). In the case of humans, evolution gave us jealousy.

It does that to prevent us from raising the wrong kids. If males did raise the wrong kids, which aren't their own, they are raising kids that doesn't have their genes, and as we know, the meaning of life in biology is passing down genes, and they'd fail in their only task in life. As a result, evolutionary theory predicts that jealousy would be more prominent in males than females.

### How it differs from Envy

Envy usually occurs when a person doesn't have another's superior quality, achievement, or possession and either desires it or wishes the other person lacked it, while jealousy, by contrast, occurs when a person fears losing an important relationship with another person to a rival (Parrot, 1993).

According to a study done by Parrot, envy is usually associated with the disapproval of the feelings itself and longing (of what the other person has), while jealousy is associated with betrayal, afraid of a possible loss, and uncertainty.

It is important to remember that they are not mutually exclusive, as one can absolutely feel angry about the unfairness of life whether they are jealous and envious.

### Why in the World Do We Get Jealous Even Though It Isn't Official?

Well, in your mind, it is. Tennov lists "acute longing for reciprocation" as a building block of limerence. The smitten person is *always* observing, constantly analyzing their crush's actions for signs of any reciprocation. A rival can threaten this hope, so they feel jealous.

What I described in jealousy section for human males are sexual jealousy, and it differs from limerent jealousy, which focuses on whether the person returns the feelings. Do not confuse these two, though limerent jealousy usually precedes sexual jealousy, and they can happen at the same time.

Tennov notes that jealousy is **NOT** a measure of limerence. You can be limerent without jealousy (a crush with no rival) or jealous without limerence (a prime example is the 4th person of the attendance in my classroom).

### Reconciliation: Daly et al.'s Evolutionary Viewpoint Differs from Yarab et al.'s Statistics.

Yeah, Daly (1982) give us the evolutionary explanation, but Yarab's stats show different finding. "What should I follow," you may ask. Well, you don't have to follow a source. You just have to use nuance.

You see, Daly was all theoretical. The why, if you will. While Yarab gives us the findings accounting with how modernization–modern culture, newer dating norms, and safer sex education–affected the results.

While males have a biological reason to have paternity uncertainty, modern dating is so far from the African Savannah that women have developed equally strong, albeit different, jealousy. Basically, the instinct is still backed by biology, but the intensity is more or less the same.

### Why Do We Want Things Other People Want?

Research has shown that we want things that other people want. This is called mimetic desire (Garrels, 2005).

First, you have to know that imitation is basically like an instict. We can see this as babies can imitate facial expressions mere minutes after born.

Plus, we have mirror neurons, which are neurons that fire when we do something and see someone else do that thing. Our brains are programmed from the start to react to other people's action that matches our own. This is essential to empathy and language, but it also makes people want to imitate others too.

Garrels points out that imitation is more goal-oriented. Babies don't simply copy movements. They copy the meaning and intentions behind it. Turns out, when two people imitate each other's desire for something, they become rivals. This is *Acquisitive Mimesis* (Garrels, 2005). And this is how you get jealousy.

## Attachment Theory

Basically, it's a theory that explains how humans attach to each other, and it's particularly influenced by early experiences (Cassidy, 2013).

Human babies are born totally helpless. Because of this, we developed attachment. Its only job is to make sure the baby stays close enough to the caregiver so they don't die, duh. In Cassidy's paper, they name it the "Attachment Behavioral System," which sounds a lot more complicated than it needs to be.

### The Parent's Job in This Scenario

In this theory, parents have two jobs (Cassidy, 2013):

- Being a secure base. If you know the base is safe and has supplies, you feel brave enough to explore the world and that you can always return to your parents.

- Being a safe haven. If something scary shows up, like hurting, being scared or stressed, you go back to your parents to hide and recharge.

Kind of sweet, isn't it?

### The Building Stage

The paper says by the time you are 1 year old (yes, that early), you already have a method (Internal Working Model) in our head on attachment based on how your parents treated you.

- If your parents treated you well, you are secure. "I'm screwed, but someone will help me. I am worth helping."

- If your parents treated you like dogcrap, you will be insecure. "I'm in a peculiar situation. I'm on my own. People are mean!"

How is this related to crushes? Because those methods stick. Really stubbornly. We have the core system built in (like instincts) but we need to build the system for attachment because we don't have one. We do this through early relationships with our caretaker, like our parents. Our brain adapts easily especially when we are young through age three (Marzola, 2023).

### The Panic Slider

We have the HPA axis, which is like slider for panic.

- If your parents calm you down, the HPA axis reduces, which teaches babies how to turn it off independently.

- Inversely, if your parents don't calm you down or neglects you, the HPA axis is stuck in the max setting, and they don't know how to turn it off yet (Gunnar & Quevedo, 2007).

  - If the baby doesn't learn how to turn it off, then they will have a hard time turning it off later in life.

  - They can neglect you by being workaholics, emotionally unavailable, or even being anxious themselves!

### Good News! You're Not Totally Screwed

This doesn't mean it's impossible to reverse bad parenting (Wallin, 2007). One can, for example, take therapy to reduce it or find healthy relationships to finally learn how to move that slider down.

### How it Affects People who Act Out

How this affects behaviors in the future is... complicated. There are a lot of papers in this stuff that sometimes disagree with each other. Like what? But thanks to some meta-analyses, we have a good idea on the effects (Fearon et al., 2010; Groh et al., 2012).

Basically, the meta-analyses show that if you're or disorganized or avoidant, you're much more likely to externalize (acting out/aggression) your issues. Surprisingly, avoidant attachments are the only one that are likely to internalize (depression/anxiety) their issues, Even though they act aloof, the math shows they're struggling the most.

That's vague, stalling, and cowardly. Instead, say, for instance

> I want to take you to a café near our school at 5 PM. Sounds good?

If they say yes, great! Otherwise, it stings a bit, but it's fine. You'll get through it. After some time, you can confess your feelings.

But beware! Your dopamine levels will drop. You will feel less thrilled (dopamine), but you will feel more loved (serotonin & oxytoncin). I think it's more worth.

### I have feelings, but I don't want to date. How to break it?

- **Literally block them on WhatsApp or Instagram**. I know it sounds like you're a jerk, but remember, this is for your wellbeing!

- **Do not accidentally try to run to them** in the hallway or anywhere else. Do not give your dopamine-addicted brain that uncertainty, otherwise it will keep pumping out dopamine but [be met with nothing](#the-prediction-error).

- **Humanize them**. Do not only think about their [positives](#2-the-halo-effect). Think about their negatives. Maybe picture them with odor. It's weird, but it works. This will make your limerence eventually die.

- **Write it down** in third person. You will experience postevent processing, which is like replaying a conversation at midnight for no reason and you want to scream, if you don't write it down in third person (Kross et al., 2014).

### I don't have feelings, and I don't want to date

Don't be afraid to talk to them and say that while you want to be friends, you are not interested in being parters towards them directly and kindly.

> Hey, I'm flattered, but I don't feel the same way. Let's be friends!

Remember, a sting of rejection is better than [affective ruminating](#why-vague-answers-are-bad). Do not keep them around for that ego boost.

#### Scenario A. We Aren't Official.

Ouch, that hurts. But it's fine. Take deep breaths. No need to fight. You will likely experience [mimetic desire](#why-do-we-want-things-other-people-want). You want to be with them more because someone else wants or are with them. If you compete now, you will have to compete forever. It is not worth your time. Go find someone else that's worth your time.

That's just called cheating! It irrevocably destroys the trust you had. If you are with this person, leave *now*. Do not interact them ever again because they're ~~likely~~ a jerk. Do not try to salvage what you had. You will be hurt more as that person is likely to be a manipulator if they're not loyal.

## Why "Maybe" is Worse Than "No"

So, you finally did it. You confessed. I guess. Congratulations? But they gave a non-committal answer.

> Oh, that's so sweet! But later? I'm actually really busy right now.

Then, you went home and cannot stop thinking about the confession.

> What the hell does that "maybe" mean? Does she like me? Does she think that I'm only an ordinary friend? AAAH!

Or you confessed online, and they left you on read.

You think about it so much it impairs your sleep. And you finally wonder, "Why am I getting all of these thoughts?"

Well, congratulations, you have just been handed an unfinished task. It's not just you; it's actually a phenomenon in humans, and we call it the Zeigarnik Effect (Zeigarnik, 1938).

### What's the Zeigarnik Effect?

Back in the 1920s (yes, that long ago), a psychologist named Bluma Zeigarnik noticed that waiters could memorize any unpaid orders perfectly *up until* the bill was paid. Like everything was forgotten. Zeigarnik found this fascinating, so she designed experiments to know more about it. Then, she wrote a paper and published it as *Über das Behalten von erledigten und unerledigten Handlungen*, or what common folks call it, *On Finished and Unfinished Tasks*.

It is a tendency where people remember unfinished tasks a lot better than finished tasks. She tested this hypothesis by doing some experiments that shows that uncompleted mental tasks (such as puzzles or constructing cardboard boxes) induce a tendency to experience intrusive thoughts and are 90% more likely to be recollected than finished tasks.

### Affective Rumination vs. Problem-Solving Pondering

Cropley & Zijlstra (2011) made it pretty clear that Affective Rumination is different from Problem-Solving Pondering.

1. Affective Rumination is basically like a state where negative, intrusive, and repeating thoughts about an unfinished think appear. "Oh my God. Did I fumble hard? Why haven't they replied? Did I look too desperate? Help!" This is unproductive and doesn't go anywhere. This is seen as the bridge that ruins sleep for employees (Syrek, 2017)

2. Problem-Solving Pondering is a form of long thinking about previous work, and how it can be improved, but does *not* involve negative thoughts. "In 3 days, if they haven't got to me back, I'll send them a bump. If they don't reply anyway, I'll just continue playing games. Shucks!"

### Why Vague Answers are Bad

When people leave work with unfinished tasks, they are more than twice as likely to spiral into Affective Rumination $\beta\_w=.18,~~SE=.05$ than to trigger the calm Problem-Solving Pondering $\beta\_w=.07,~~SE=.04$ (Syrek, 2017). Consequently, if someone confesses to you, and you give a vague answer, that person literally cannot sleep because they might be ruminating about it.

### So, What?

It's pretty easy. You should give a direct Yes/No answer. You can think for a while, but not defer it until the following days. And by definition, if you write your thoughts on a piece of paper, it will kill this effect.

## The Triangular Theory of Love

No, it's not a love triangle. This is different. Sternberg's (1986) triangular theory of love is a theory to describe love. It's just that. The vertices of the triangle are defined as:

- **Intimacy**: This is the emotional vertex. The closeness, the warmth, and sharing secret. It's the bestie part.

- **Passion**: This is the motivation vertex. It's the butterflies and the physical attraction.

- **Commitment**: This is the cognitive vertex. It's when you decide consciously if you want to commit to it. It means during the short term, you choose to love that person, and during the long term, you choose to stick around.

Using that, we can define 8 types of love.

- **Nonlove** (-): Strangers.

- **Liking** (Intimacy): Your friends.

- **Infatuated Love** (Passion): Everything we discussed. Like cmon.

- **Empty Love** (Commitment): Marriages where everyone does nothing.

- **Romantic Love** (Intimacy + Passion): High school sweetheart rom-com moment.

- **Companionate Love** (Intimacy + Commitment): Deep, long-term friendship. Very wholesome. Also found in a happy, long-term marriage.

- **Fatuous Love** (Passion + Commitment): Instant romance. You meet, get obsessed, exclusively date after one week, get married in one month, and then realize you are an idiot. Fatuous meaning foolish.

- **Consummate Love** (Passsion + Intimacy + Commitment): Nobody I know of. It's perfect.

It's not discrete as it is continuous. What it means is that you can have a bit of something but also a lot of something at the same time. Think of it as like the shape of a triangle. If it's equilateral, it's balance. Otherwise, if it's a scalene, something is off and unbalanced. And the area of the triangle is the total amount.

Satisfaction is linked through your expectation and reality. If the reality of the triangle matches your expectations of the triangle, you will be more satisfied. And if you feel you are 100% commited but your partner feels otherwise, it will fail.

## The Situationship

According to Langlais et al. (2024), situationships are defined as a "romantic relationship with no clarity or label, low levels of commitments, but similar romantic behaviors as established couples by means of affecton, sexual behaviors, and time spent together." In short, it's where you put in the effort of a partner but none of the security and without label. Look familiar? You might be going through one right now. Feeling all lovey-dovey but never actually having the talk. Wait–

Usually, if things go well, crushes (passion) turns to romantic love (passion + intimacy). If you also went through the talk, you will get consummate love (passion + intimacy + commitment). In situationships, you are stuck at romantic love for a long time. Longer than it should be. So long that it hurts and you *will* [ruminate](#why-maybe-is-worse-than-no). Langlais calls this "Stagnant Romantic Love."

And especially when Langlais et al. compared situationships to actual couples. They are little to no difference in the actual intimacy, like kissing or spending quality time together. And even then, people in situationships have significantly lower satisfaction and lower levels of trust.

In short, do not do this in high school, especially when you are in high school. Relevant reasons why can be found at [Error Management Theory](#how-a-crush-works), [Intermittent Reinforcement](#what-does-gambling-have-to-do-with-crushes), and the [Zeigarnik Effect](#whats-the-zeigarnik-effect). ## How to Know if That Person has a Crush On Me?

You want to look for body language. Specifically, subconscious signs of proximity and mirroring.

1. Proximity. If you are in a room of 50 people and there's always one that just seems to be around you once, it's a coincidence. Twice, you got unlucky. Thrice and more? That's a pattern.

2. Mirroring. Remember the [mirror neurons](#why-do-we-want-things-other-people-want)? Well, they will **subconsciously do what you do**. At that section, I wrote that the roles of the mirror neurons is to enhance social connection and empathy. Usually, at least. When you lean back, they lean back. When you drink water, they drink too. They are unconsciously trying to tell you, "I feel safe with you. I want to be like you." On hindsight, that's super sweet!

   - A study placed participants on an online date (Li et al., 2024). When the researchers artificially increased their partner's smile, the participant thought, "Hey, this person likes me a lot!"

   - When both are smiling at the same time, romantic attraction were the highest.

   - Interestingly, when both people had decreased smiles, it was still rated higher than when one is smiling and the other is not. Wrong energy kills the vibe and makes things awkward!

3. You might think that the eyebrows are useless for romantic attraction, but a study found that our eyebrows are specifically designed for social connection (Godinho et al., 2018). For instance, when someone **raised their eyebrow briefly** when they notice you, they are likely surprised and pleased that you were there.

They are also quite open, as in getting unique ideas to feel superior, not to genuinely contribute. Plus, they think they are smarter than they are. Examples include Ke–umm... nothing.

2. Machiavellianism. "I'm silent. I am at the back of the class. And I am planning your demise. I will manipulate you for my advantage. I know what I'm doing." They are cold and cynical, and they *use* people. I think these guys are scary. Like real scary. Because they are hard to spot. They are NOT nice whatsoever and have even lower agreeableness.
